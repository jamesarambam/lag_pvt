## Meeting
---

Please consider meetings with your advisor as a resource, and come prepared to maximize its usefulness.

The following structure is helpful:

1. Use slides (Google Docs or PowerPoint). If you have any figures or plots to show, it is better to put them on the slides before the meeting. Avoid running experiments and generating plots during meetings, as it is not an effective use of meeting time.

2. Recap: what was the state of your work at the last meeting, and what did we agree you would do for the next meeting?

3. Discuss your progress, problems, ideas

4. Propose what to do next until the next meeting

Your advisors want to give you helpful feedback. However, remember that advisors have many such meetings, and keeping all the details in mind between meetings can be challenging. So, to facilitate valuable feedback, it is helpful to remind advisors of the state of the work and current targets.

Preparing for the meeting means that you give some thought to 3) and 4) before the meeting. During the meeting, you should note down all work tasks agreed on, which can be used for 2) at the next meeting.

If you want more detailed technical feedback on an idea, it is best to send a write-up before the meeting. Writing it up is also immensely helpful for clarifying your perspective.

<br>

## Feedback
---

One of the most critical functions an advisor provides to you is feedback on your ideas, methods, results, writing, etc. Consider the following:

1. If you want to discuss a technical idea at a meeting, it would be very helpful if you send a write-up of your idea before the meeting so that he can take the time to prepare. At least try to think of an organized presentation of your idea on the whiteboard (or slides) for the meeting so that he can follow it step by step.

2. If he gives you written feedback on a document you prepared, please take the feedback seriously. You should assume that when you send your next draft, he will check whether and how you addressed the comments. Give your reasons if you decided not to address a more major comment. Feedback is a two-way conversation. You should actively ask questions rather than just passively receive feedback. If the feedback doesn't address a particular point that is important to you, then you should always feel free to ask for more feedback on specific points.

3. It is a good idea to seek feedback from peers and experienced academics actively. Have an open attitude towards feedback rather than be defensive. This does not mean that you have to accept unfounded criticism or that you have to change your ideas and methods if you disagree with the feedback. However, think carefully about any feedback you get and whether you want to change aspects of your work to address the feedback. This may mean extra work, but the improvements can make the difference between an accepted and rejected paper.

<br>

## Assessment
---

Besides the annual review milestones required by the Department/Institute, there can be an additional short-term and long-term criteria to assess your progress:

1. After each meeting, there is an agreed set of tasks to work on until the following meeting. When you meet again, you tell the advisor about your progress on these tasks, giving an indication of your short-term progress.

2. In the long term, your PhD research objectives are an essential indicator of your progress. How much closer are you now to achieving your research objectives than you were at the start of your PhD?

3. In addition to the above, there is also an assessment on how you develop your soft skills: the quality of your writing and verbal communication, how you handle feedback and criticism, your ability to give research talks, how you work with others in the group, etc
